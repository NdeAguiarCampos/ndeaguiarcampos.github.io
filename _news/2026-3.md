What an exciting opportunity it was to present part of my PhD thesis at the [62nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Tropical Biology and Conservation (ATBC2026)](https://www.atbc2026.org/), in beautiful Xishuangbanna! I was humbled by the invitation from Prof Chris Smith-Martin and Dr Laura Boeschoten to deliver a talk at their ATBC2026 symposium "Effects of drought on tropical forest function: insights from observational studies and through fall exclusion experiments". My study was entitled **"Reliance on stem water storage maintains tropical rainforest tree function under drought"**, and the abstract can be found below.

Although this study is still in progress, I shared with the audience some preliminary estimations of the size of stem water storage pools and reliance of tropical rainforest trees on them. The fieldwork component of this study, conducted in the Daintree Drought experiment, involved installing multiple sensors on the stems of the trees, which collected data at very short time intervals (every 10 or 15 minutes). More specifically, I used sap flow sensors, point dendrometers and xylem psychrometers to track fine-scale water movements on the trees. The results indicate that the drought has changed the physiology of the trees but not severely impacted their ecological function.

**Abstract:** Tropical forest tree functioning underpins essential ecosystem services such as climate regulation and water cycling, yet it is increasingly threatened by more frequent and intense droughts. One mechanism by which tall trees may buffer short-term water deficits is through the use of internal water reserves stored in stem tissues. However, whether prolonged drought alters the extent to which trees rely on stem water storage (SWS) to meet daily water demands remains poorly understood. Quantifying species-specific reliance on SWS is essential to identify hydraulic traits that support drought resilience. Here, we examined how experimental drought influences the role of SWS in mature tropical rainforest trees. In a 10-year throughfall exclusion experiment in the Daintree Rainforest (northeastern Australia), we quantified SWS dynamics in droughted and control trees of three species representing early- and late-successional strategies. We combined sap flow sensors installed at basal and crown positions, point dendrometers and diurnal leaf water potential measurements to infer the magnitude and timing of SWS use. Preliminary analyses detected no significant time lags between crown and basal sap flow. However, droughted individuals showed deeper declines in leaf water potential and earlier daily stem contraction compared with control trees, suggesting increased reliance on SWS to buffer water potential drops. Moreover, the diurnal course of inferred SWS use, estimated from hourly differences between crown and basal sap flow, varied across species. By combining experimental drought manipulation with hydraulic monitoring at high temporal and spatial resolutions, this study provides new insights into species-specific drought responses that may help improve mechanistic models of tropical forest vulnerability under climate change.
