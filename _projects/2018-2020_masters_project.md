**Description:** Seasonally dry tropical forests (SDTFs) are among the world's most threatened tropical ecosystems, yet the ecological and evolutionary processes underlying their biodiversity, functioning and conservation remain poorly understood. This dissertation investigated how environmental heterogeneity and evolutionary history shape the composition, structure and ecosystem functioning of Brazilian SDTFs, with emphasis on limestone outcrops and regional biomass patterns. Tree community, soil and functional data were collected across fine-scale habitats associated with a limestone outcrop and across 313 plots distributed among 16 SDTF fragments in central-eastern Brazil. Taxonomic, functional and phylogenetic approaches were combined with analyses of community composition, phylogenetic beta-diversity, above-ground biomass, soil properties and phylogenetic signal to evaluate the influence of habitat variation and evolutionary constraints on forest assembly and carbon storage. Fine-scale habitat variation within limestone outcrops promoted marked taxonomic, functional and phylogenetic differentiation, largely driven by soil fertility and texture. Communities on the outcrop surface harboured distinct and recently diversified lineages, supporting the hypothesis that limestone environments function as litho-refugia for SDTF flora {% cite de2020can %}. At the regional scale, above-ground biomass and tree size exhibited strong phylogenetic signal, with carbon stocks concentrated in a few closely related lineages, whereas most soil preferences showed little evolutionary conservatism except for phosphorus {% cite de2021evolutionary %}. Together, these findings demonstrate that both local environmental filtering and deep evolutionary history are fundamental drivers of SDTF diversity and ecosystem functioning. Conserving the environmental heterogeneity of limestone landscapes is essential for maintaining unique evolutionary lineages, while protecting forests containing high-biomass clades is critical for preserving regional carbon stocks. This integrated perspective highlights the importance of incorporating evolutionary and habitat-based approaches into conservation planning for tropical dry forests under accelerating environmental change.

My Master's project relied on a vast forest plot dataset maintained by the [LEAF Lab](https://www.lableaf.com.br/), run by Professor Rubens Santos at the Federal University of Lavras. Due to the strongly analytical nature of this work, I developed significant skills in data analysis in R, including statistical modelling and quantifying different facets of diversity: taxonomic, functional and phylogenetic. I also had the opportunity to develop scientific writing skills, which led to publish two papers as a result of the thesis {% cite de2020can de2021evolutionary %} I also had a great lot of fun collecting additional field data in limestone-associated dry forests (photos below)!
